How many mana sources does a Commander deck need?

We simulated every mana base from 35 to 55 sources (lands, Llanowar-style dorks and Signet-style rocks) across 11 deck curves and 8 card-draw scenarios. About 190 million simulated games later, here is what holds up.

Most decks draw extra cards once their engines are online. The scenarios add cards on turns 5 to 8, from none to 7 extra cards.

With few extra cards, the deck runs out of spells: more lands and less ramp do best. With lots of extra cards, ramp pays off because there is always something to cast. A base around 42 sources is never far from the best in either case, and that is what “resilient” means here.

Dorks or rocks?

A zoom on 45 bases with 20,000 games each, averaged over all curves and scenarios.

Dorks come out ahead because they cost 1 mana instead of 2. But nothing in this model kills a creature, so in real games with board wipes, rocks are safer. Treat this table as “cheap ramp beats expensive ramp”, not “play elves over Signets”.

A trap we found: the fixed total budget

The first version fixed the total mana cost of the 99 cards (130 to 230). That sounds fair, but every land you add takes no budget, so the remaining spells get more expensive. Since the model counts all mana as equally useful, a 5-drop scores like two 2.5-drops, and the “winner” runs 47 to 55 lands.

That is why the explorer fixes the average spell cost instead: adding a source then removes a spell of the same size, which is the real trade-off when you build a deck.

How the simulation plays

  • The deck: 99 cards plus a 4-mana commander. Dorks cost 1 and tap the next turn. Rocks cost 2 and tap right away. Spells all cost the curve's average, rounded up or down so the total is exact.
  • The hand: keep 2 to 5 lands (or 1 land and 2 ramp). The first mulligan is free; the second puts a card on the bottom.
  • Each turn: draw (everyone draws on turn 1 in multiplayer), play a land, cast the commander as soon as there are 4 mana, cast ramp first through turn 6, then spend as much mana as possible on spells.
  • Measured: mana made, mana spent on real spells (the commander counts, ramp doesn't), unused mana, spells cast, and spells stuck in hand, through turn 10 and through turn 8.
  • Fair comparison: every deck plays the same shuffles, so small gaps between decks are real, not luck. 5,000 games per deck, curve and scenario.
  • Resilience: for each curve and scenario we find the best deck, then measure how far behind every other deck is. The most resilient deck has the smallest gap in its worst scenario.

What this model gets wrong

It is a goldfish: no opponents and no real cards. These gaps matter, biggest first.

  • All mana counts the same. Mana on turn 3 matters far more than mana on turn 10, but here they score equally.
  • Extra cards are free. Real card draw costs mana, and real decks have mana sinks. Here the hand is nearly empty by turn 10, so “unused mana” is overstated.
  • The curve is flat. Every spell costs the same. A real hand stuck with three 6-drops never happens here.
  • Averages hide the bad games. How often you miss your third land drop says more than the mean.
  • Smaller gaps: no removal on dorks, no Sol Ring or tapped lands, no colors, a simple mulligan rule.
Next step: rerun with the real curves of our decks, give early turns more weight, and report how often each base stumbles. Our earlier study found that win rate follows how fast a deck deploys, not how much mana it makes in total.

Run it yourself

The simulator is plain Node, no install. A full sweep takes about 3 minutes on 4 cores.

cd tools/sim/manabase
node manasim.js sweep --mode avg --games 5000     # every base, fixed curve
node manasim.js sweep --mode budget --games 5000  # the fixed-budget version
node manasim.js one --mode avg --config 32:5:5 --budget 3.25 --games 20000
node analyze.js results-sweep-avg-tight.json --metric useful8
